Q1. Your fraud detection model has 98% accuracy but your manager is unhappy. Why?

The model is likely predicting everything as "not fraud." With only 2% fraud cases, always predicting non-fraud gives 98% accuracy but 0% fraud detection — which is completely useless. I'd immediately look at the confusion matrix and switch to F1-score and Recall as evaluation metrics, since catching actual fraud (recall) matters more than overall accuracy.


Q2. You applied SMOTE but your model performance got worse. What went wrong?

Most likely, SMOTE was applied before train-test split or outside the pipeline — causing data leakage. Synthetic samples generated from the full dataset bleed information into the test set. The fix is to always apply SMOTE inside a pipeline so it only runs on training folds during cross-validation, never touching the test set.


Q3. You have a medical dataset — 99% healthy, 1% cancer. What's your approach?

Here recall for the cancer class is critical — a false negative (missing cancer) is far worse than a false positive. I would use class_weight='balanced', apply SMOTE, lower the decision threshold below 0.5 to maximize recall, and evaluate using PR-AUC. I'd also use StratifiedKFold to ensure each fold maintains the 1% ratio.


Q4. Why not just duplicate minority samples instead of SMOTE?

Simple duplication causes overfitting on minority samples — the model just memorizes those exact points. SMOTE creates synthetic samples by interpolating between existing minority instances, adding diversity and helping the model generalize better to unseen minority cases.


Q5. When would you prefer undersampling over oversampling?

When the majority class is extremely large (millions of samples) and computation is a constraint. Undersampling reduces training time significantly. However, it risks losing important majority class patterns. A combined approach like SMOTETomek is usually safer — it oversamples minority while removing noisy majority samples near the boundary.


Q6. Your model has high recall but very low precision on minority class. What does that mean and is it acceptable?

High recall means we're catching most actual positives but low precision means many false alarms. Whether it's acceptable depends on business context. In cancer detection — yes, acceptable, missing a case is worse. In spam filtering — no, flagging too many legitimate emails as spam is harmful. Always align the precision-recall tradeoff with business cost of each error type.


Q7. How do you handle imbalance in a multi-class problem?

Use average='macro' or average='weighted' in F1-score to evaluate all classes fairly. Apply SMOTE per class using SMOTE with k_neighbors tuned per class size. Set class_weight='balanced' in the model. For severe imbalance, treat it as One-vs-Rest binary classification per class.
